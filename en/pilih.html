PSU calculator

How many watts do you really need?

Pick the components, then see what uses the most power. The answer is almost always the GPU and CPU. RAM and SSDs only use a few watts.

Recommended PSU
650W

If the PSU is too small

  • The PC suddenly shuts off or restarts during heavy games or rendering, because the PSU's protection cuts the power.
  • Blue screens (BSOD) or the GPU "disappearing" from the system.
  • The PSU runs at 100% constantly: hot, noisy, and quick to fail.

If the PSU is bigger than needed

  • It's not dangerous. The PC only draws the power it needs.
  • Your electricity bill doesn't go up because of the PSU's wattage. What sets the bill is what the components actually use.
  • The only downsides are a higher purchase price and slightly lower efficiency at very low loads.

Power spikes

Modern GPUs can spike above their rated wattage for fractions of a millisecond. That's why NVIDIA recommends a PSU much larger than the card's own wattage. For example, a 250 W RTX 5070 is recommended with a 650 W PSU.

What a weak GPU feels like

The screen below imitates real in-game symptoms. Pick a GPU class, then move your mouse or finger across the screen to steer. The graph under the screen is the frame time: a flat line means smooth, tall spikes mean stutter.

Choppy (low FPS)

The GPU can't draw enough frames per second. Below 30 FPS, motion feels like a slide show. 60 FPS feels smooth, and 144 FPS feels very fluid, but only on a 144 Hz monitor.

Blurry or blocky

To raise FPS, games lower the render resolution and stretch it to the screen. The result looks soft and blurry. DLSS and FSR are clever ways to do this with AI so it isn't as blurry.

Stutter

The picture suddenly freezes for a split second. The most common causes are full GPU VRAM (textures too large), full RAM, or the game loading data from slow storage.

Online lag ≠ GPU

Turn on the 250 ms ping option. The picture stays smooth, but the car is late following your hand. That's an internet connection or server problem, not the graphics card. An expensive GPU won't cure high ping.

Peripherals

Monitors, keyboards, UPSs, and networking

An office PC package isn't complete without these. They're also often where the profit margin and the client complaints come from.

Monitor

  • Panel: IPS (accurate colour, common for offices and design), VA (high contrast, deep blacks), TN (cheap, washed-out colour), OLED (best, most expensive).
  • Resolution: Full HD 1920×1080 suits 22–24", QHD 2560×1440 suits 27", 4K 3840×2160 for design and video.
  • Refresh rate: 60–100 Hz for offices, 144–180 Hz for gaming.
  • Ports: make sure they match the PC. Older PCs may only have VGA or DVI.
  • Price: 24" FHD IPS office monitor Rp1.3–2.5M · 27" QHD 165 Hz Rp3–5M · OLED Rp8M and up.

Keyboard and mouse

  • Membrane: quiet and cheap, the office standard.
  • Mechanical: each key has its own switch, more durable and nicer to type on, but louder.
  • Wireless: a 2.4 GHz dongle (stable, plug-and-play) or Bluetooth (no dongle, sometimes needs re-pairing).
  • For office rentals: a wired USB set causes the fewest problems and is easiest to replace.

UPS

  • A 5–15 minute backup battery during power cuts, enough to save work and shut the PC down properly.
  • Types: line-interactive (with AVR) is the most common for office PCs. Online UPSs are for servers and sensitive equipment.
  • VA isn't watts: a 650 VA UPS can usually only handle about 325–390 W. Add up the watts of the PC + monitor, then pick a UPS whose watt rating is higher.
  • Modern PSUs (active PFC) are safest with a pure sine wave UPS. Cheap UPSs output a stepped wave, which on some PSUs can make the PC shut off when it switches to battery.
  • Price: 650–800 VA Rp0.7–1.2M · 1200 VA Rp1.5–2.5M · pure sine 1500 VA Rp3–6M.

Stabilizers and grounding

  • A stabilizer evens out rising and falling voltage, but doesn't help when the power goes out.
  • Where the power often dips or surges, a line-interactive UPS already includes this AVR function.
  • Make sure the office wiring has an earth (ground). Without it, the case can give a tingle and equipment is more easily damaged by lightning.

Networking

  • Wi-Fi: Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac), Wi-Fi 6 (ax), Wi-Fi 6E (adds the 6 GHz band), Wi-Fi 7 (be). For busy offices, Wi-Fi 6 and up is much more stable.
  • LAN: 1 Gbps is standard, 2.5 Gbps is becoming common on new motherboards. Desk PCs are better on a cable, which is more stable than Wi-Fi.
  • Switch: adds more LAN ports. Router: shares the internet. Access point: extends Wi-Fi coverage.

Webcams and headsets

  • Online meetings are routine now. A 1080p webcam and a headset with a mic sound far clearer than built-in mics.
  • USB headsets don't depend on the PC's audio jack quality and generally cause fewer sound problems.
